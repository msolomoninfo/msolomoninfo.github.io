Was It Worth It?

A check-in app that interrupts the scroll to ask one honest question and what nine people taught us when we put it in front of them.

Role
UX Designer and Researcher
Timeline
8 weeks
Team
Solo Project
Tools
Figma, UX Tweak
Overview

The Objective

Most people don't open TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube Shorts meaning to stay long. They open it to disengage for a minute and the platforms are built to make sure that minute doesn't end on its own.

These feeds are algorithmically tuned to remove natural stopping points, turning a short mental break into an hour of scrolling nobody chose. The cost shows up elsewhere: lost sleep, broken focus, less time with the people in the room. Existing screen-time dashboards and app blockers exist, but they're easy to dismiss and don't address the moment that actually matters how the session felt once it was over.

Primary audience: young adults, 18–28, largely college students and early-career professionals the group with the highest reported daily usage and the most on the line when scrolling collides with a deadline or a night's sleep.

Constraints: tested against a deliberately wide age range (24–70) beyond that primary audience, to stress-test the concept against more than one relationship to social media.

Research

Discovery

Rather than just measuring screen time, "Was It Worth It?" gives users a recap the moment they leave a social app: how long they scrolled, and one simple question did that actually make you feel better than when you opened it? Over time, the pattern becomes visible.

To find out whether people could actually use it without guidance, a mixed-method usability study ran across nine participants spanning ages 24 to 70 (mean age 39) a deliberately wide range, from retirees to early-career professionals, to stress-test the design against more than one relationship to social media.

Research methods:

  • Round 1 — low fidelity wireframes, moderated with 6 testers
  • Round 2 — clickable hi-fi prototype, moderated with 7 new testers

What We Found

Three problems stood between people and the point of the app

All three findings trace back to the same root cause: the prototype asks people to reflect, but doesn't yet give them a stable, legible place to do it.

Critical

The Reflections screen reads backwards

Task 4 asked participants to find which platform left them feeling worst. Just 43% picked correctly the layout's visual hierarchy pointed people toward the opposite conclusion the data actually supported, and there was no confirming state once they'd "finished" the task.

57%answered incorrectly
43%identified the right platform
0confirmation or next step after the task
High

People get stuck looping between the same screens

With no persistent "home" or exit, first-time users defaulted to backtracking whenever they got lost, hoping to reset. Task 2 logging how a session felt took the longest of any task on average, largely because it was most people's first real encounter with the flow.

1m 32saverage time on Task 2
0persistent exit or home affordance
High

Two entry points look like one

Task 1 simulates being interrupted mid-scroll, inside a social app; Task 2 simulates opening the check-in on your own, after you've already closed it. Participants repeatedly couldn't tell the two apart the visual language didn't distinguish "the app caught me" from "I came here myself," so people applied the wrong mental model to each.

"The interface's current state is riddled with navigation gaps — but the flows and visual language underneath are worth building on."
Recommendations

What each finding points to

FindingRecommendation
Reflections is unreadable Reformat around the single actionable insight, simplify the platform comparison so the "worse" platform is visually obvious, and add clear navigation once the user has seen it.
Users get stuck looping Add a confirmation state after every completed action and a persistent exit or home control on every screen, so backtracking is never the only way out.
Entry points look identical Give the interrupted-mid-scroll flow and the self-initiated check-in distinct visual treatments, so the context is legible before the user reads a word.
What Surprised Us

Two findings that weren't on the research plan

It's not just social media

Older participants pushed back on the framing. Their doomscrolling wasn't TikTok it was news sites and the links that follow them out of an email inbox. The underlying pattern, an algorithm-shaped absence of a stopping point, isn't unique to social platforms. It suggests the eventual scope of the tool may need to widen past the apps it started with.

Reflection can read as guilt

Only 28.6% of participants thought a repeated "was it worth it?" prompt would change their behavior over time most expected to eventually dismiss it like any other notification, and several said it felt more like guilt than guidance. And yet every single participant admitted social media keeps them scrolling longer than they mean to. The demand for the tool is real; the current delivery of the question isn't yet landing the way it's meant to.

What Surprised Us

Two findings that weren't on the research plan

People want help stepping back from the scroll. The current prototype proves the idea it just hasn't yet built the interface calm enough, and clear enough, to earn that reflection instead of demanding it.

Next: rebuild Reflections around one legible insight, give every screen a way out, and rethink the prompt itself so it reads as an offer, not a scold.
