Overview

The Objective

Wink is a dating platform built around compatibility over quantity. Instead of a bottomless stack of strangers, its matching algorithm scores compatibility from a user's answers to hundreds of questions about interests and core values, then surfaces a smaller pool of genuinely fitting matches.

The goal wasn't just a prettier swipe screen, it was a more personalized and inclusive experience for people who'd grown tired of superficial matching, ghosting, and apps that didn't reflect who they actually are.

Constraints: a solo project with no dedicated engineering team every research, design, and testing decision had to hold up on its own before any handoff.

My role: research, personas, wireframes, visual design, and both rounds of usability testing.

Research

Discovery

Three people, three very different reasons to be tired of dating apps. Before any screens got designed, personas were built around the pain points that kept coming up: matches that go nowhere, time wasted on the wrong fits, and apps that weren't built with everyone in mind.

  • Round 1: black and white wireframes, moderated with 5 testers
  • Round 2: clickable hi-fi prototype, moderated with 5 new testers

What informal testing found

  • The report/block flow (Task 2) took the longest — testers weren't sure where to find it.
  • Some of the learning curve was the study format itself: several testers had never done a usability session before.
  • Without color, the wireframe was genuinely hard to read for some participants.

What formal testing found

  • The filter icon was regularly mistaken for account blocking or reporting — iconography needed to be clearer.
  • The report form didn't offer enough options to capture what testers actually wanted to flag.
  • A few prototype-only issues (unresponsive touch targets, a message icon that blended into the background) got flagged for fixing.

Iteration

Closing the gaps testing surfaced

Every formal-testing finding turned into a specific design change — nothing got fixed without a tester pointing at exactly why it was confusing

  • Enlarged and added contrast to iconography so it reads clearly against the pink background.
  • Increased touch target sizes across buttons to cut down interaction errors.
  • Redesigned the messaging icon so it no longer blends into a matched profile.
  • Rebuilt the report form to be more interactive, with clear support for selecting multiple reasons.
  • Added confirmation feedback whenever a user takes an important action, like reporting or matching.
Iteration

What two rounds of testing actually proved

The gap between the two testing rounds ended up being the most useful data point in the whole project. It wasn't just that the hi-fi prototype looked more finished testers moved through it in a third of the time it took to get through the same tasks on paper, and rated it 9+ out of 10 on both experience and intuitiveness.

Some of that gap was fidelity: color, real photography, and a tactile phone screen are simply easier to navigate than pencil on an index card. But a real part of it was the design itself catching up to what round-one testers were struggling with especially the block-and-report flow, which went from the slowest, most confusing task to one of the fastest.

The lesson I'm carrying forward: test the safety-critical flows first. Reporting and blocking aren't a feature users browse casually they're the flow someone reaches for at their most uncomfortable moment, and that's exactly where clarity matters most.
